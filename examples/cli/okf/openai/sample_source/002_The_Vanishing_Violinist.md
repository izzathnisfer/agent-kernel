# The Vanishing Violinist

## I.

It is a curious fact that the most public disappearance I ever witnessed happened in front of nine hundred people, under a bright stage light, with Inspector Ada Merrow seated four rows from the front and myself beside her.

The violinist was Miklós Varga, the most celebrated soloist to visit our city in a decade. He walked out to the centre of the Athenaeum stage, lifted his bow, and — between the conductor's downbeat and the first note — was simply gone. No trapdoor, the theatre swore. No smoke. One moment a man in black, the next an empty pool of light and a violin lying on the boards as though set down by a ghost.

"Well," said Merrow, sitting up for the first time all evening. "That was worth the ticket."

## II.

The manager was frantic; the audience, once the shouting subsided, was thrilled. Merrow ignored both and went straight to the abandoned instrument. She did not touch it. She crouched, tilted her head, and studied the way it lay.

"A man who faints drops his violin," she said. "A man who is seized drops it and it skids. This one was placed — bridge up, bow laid parallel. Varga set it down himself, carefully, because he intends to play it again. Wherever he has gone, he means to come back."

"Then it is a trick? A stunt?"

"It is a disappearance he arranged and someone else improved upon." She rose. "Note the rosin, Vane. There is fresh rosin dust on the boards a full stride to the left of where the violin lies. He stood there, not here, when the light found him. The light we all watched was aimed at a mark he had already left."

## III.

The trick, Merrow reconstructed, was mirror and misdirection: a black-draped frame, angled to throw the empty rear of the stage forward as though it were the front, so that the audience stared at a reflection while the man himself stepped quietly into the wings. A harmless illusion, rehearsed for the sensation of it.

But Varga had not reappeared for his bow, and that was the part no illusion explained.

"His manager sold the trick to the newspapers a week ago," Merrow said, reading a crumpled clipping from the dressing-room bin. "In confidence, of course. Which means a certain reader knew exactly where a famous man would step, unseen, at a precise minute." She looked up. "Bright things, Vane, and only what cannot be replaced. Varga owns the Aubert Guarneri — the one violin in this hemisphere worth stealing. And he left it on the stage."

## IV.

We found him in the scene dock, bound but unhurt, exactly as the lighthouse keeper had been left — inconvenienced, not injured. His pockets had been emptied of a single item: the key to the strongroom where the true Guarneri was kept, the instrument on stage being a fine copy he played only for the disappearing trick, precisely so the original need never leave its case.

"She has gone for the strongroom," Merrow said. "She always takes the one that cannot be replaced, and never the one left conveniently to hand. It is almost a courtesy. Come."

## V.

We reached the strongroom to find it open, empty, and immaculate — no force, no mess, the false violin from the stage left inside in place of the true one, as though the Magpie could not bear to leave a shelf looking robbed.

Pinned to the velvet was a calling card, blank but for a single line in a fine hand: *A fair trade, Inspector — a copy for an original. You of all people should approve.*

Merrow laughed aloud in the empty vault. "She read the newspaper trick and built her own around it. Do you see, Vane? I keep her case file to learn her taste. She reads my city to learn its brightest things. We are each other's almanac." She pocketed the card with the others. "Varga will play the copy tonight and no one will know the difference — no one but her, and me, and now you."
