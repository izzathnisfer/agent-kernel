# The Clockmaker's Alibi

## I.

The case of the clockmaker turned on a single minute, and it was Inspector Ada Merrow's peculiar gift that she distrusted minutes more than any other evidence a court will admit.

Old Absolom Reeve kept the finest clock shop on Candle Street, and he was found dead among his own movements one Sunday morning, a fallen ladder beside him and a high shelf swept clean. An accident, plainly — save that his nephew and sole heir, Julian, had an alibi so precise it might have been machined. At the moment of death, Julian swore, he stood across the city at the Exchange, and forty witnesses had heard the great Exchange clock strike ten as they spoke with him.

"Forty witnesses," said Merrow, "and one clock. I am interested in the clock."

## II.

The shop was a forest of ticking. Merrow moved through it slowly, and I saw her doing the thing she does when a room is lying to her: listening, with her eyes shut, to whether its many voices agreed.

"They are all correct," she said at last, and it sounded like an accusation. "Every clock in a dead horologist's shop, wound and true, to the second. A sick man lets his clocks drift. A busy man lets one or two run fast. Only a man who wished a particular time to be beyond dispute would set forty clocks in perfect agreement — and then die beneath them."

She knelt by the body, then by the fallen ladder, and grunted. "The ladder did not fall. It was laid down. See the felt pads on its feet — no scuff, no gouge in the floor wax. A ladder that topples with a man leaves a mark. This one was placed as tidily as a violin on a stage."

## III.

The nephew's alibi was the Exchange clock, and so to the Exchange we went. Merrow climbed to the movement room behind the great dial and examined the escapement with a jeweller's loupe.

"There," she said. "The regulator has been moved and moved back — the lever is bright where a thumb has worried it. Someone advanced this clock by three minutes last week, let the city hear it strike, and then corrected it before the keeper's Monday inspection." She sat back on her heels. "Julian did not need to be in two places. He needed only the whole city to believe ten o'clock came three minutes before it did. His forty witnesses are honest. They are simply forty minutes wrong, all in the same direction, because they trusted the one clock a clockmaker's heir knew how to reach."

## IV.

We took Julian Reeve that afternoon, and he confessed with the sullen pride of a man annoyed to be caught by his own trade. He had killed his uncle for the shop, staged the ladder, and bought himself three minutes of alibi by tampering with the clock the whole city set its watches by.

But there was a second theft inside the first, and it was Merrow who saw it. The high shelf swept clean in the murdered man's shop had held the Reeve tourbillon — a single pocket watch, a lifetime's masterpiece, worth more than the building beneath it.

"Julian did not take it," Merrow said. "He barely knew it existed. But someone read of Reeve's death in the Monday early edition and understood, as Julian never could, that the one irreplaceable thing in that shop now stood on an unguarded shelf."

## V.

The tourbillon was gone by the time we returned, and in its place — of course — a card, and on the card a single line: *You caught the clumsy one, Inspector. Leave me the beautiful one. C.D.*

I confess I lost my temper on Reeve's behalf. Merrow did not.

"She solved my murder for me by taking the watch," she said mildly, adding the card to the small stack she would never show a superior. "Had the shelf been full when we arrived, I might have believed the ladder after all. The Magpie only ever steals what cannot be replaced — and in doing so she tells me, every time, precisely which thing in the room was worth killing for. I do not forgive her, Vane. But I have stopped pretending she is not useful."
