# The Harbour Master's Ledger

## I.

Of all the cases I shared with Inspector Ada Merrow, the affair of the harbour master's ledger is the one she calls, with no false modesty, the only time she and the Magpie were ever on the same side — and neither of them said so aloud.

It began with a drowning that was not a drowning. The harbour master, a hard and honest man named Corrigan, was pulled from the dock basin at dawn. The coroner was ready to write *misadventure*, a night, a bottle, a bad step. Merrow read the man's ledger instead, and the ledger told a different story than the water did.

## II.

"A harbour master's ledger is a confession no one thinks to hide," she told me, turning the salt-stiffened pages. "Every ship, every cargo, every fee. Corrigan's hand is steady for three years and then, these last two months, it shakes. Not with drink — the letters are careful, too careful, the hand of a sober man frightened of his own record." She tapped a column. "He was logging cargoes that never sailed. Someone was running goods through this harbour under false manifests, and Corrigan was made to sign for them."

"Smuggling."

"Worse. Substitution. Look — the *Marguerite* enters carrying tin and leaves carrying tin, same weight, same fee. But tin does not need a guard of four men on the night watch, and Corrigan notes four men, every time, in the margin where he thought no inspector would read." She closed the book. "They were shipping something that had to look like nothing. And Corrigan learned what it was."

## III.

We traced the false manifests to a shipping agent named Prentice, a smooth man with soft hands and a warehouse he was very reluctant to open. When at last we did — with a magistrate's paper and Merrow's patience both exhausted — we found it stripped bare, swept as clean as a clockmaker's shelf, and a night watchman nursing a headache and a story about a slight figure in oilskins who had come and gone before midnight.

"She was here before us," I said.

"She was here *instead* of us," Merrow corrected. "Prentice was not smuggling tin, Vane. He was smuggling the estate of half the drowned and the dead of this county — plate, jewels, a museum's worth of bright irreplaceable things, bought cheap from grieving families and shipped abroad to be sold as if clean. It is the ugliest trade I know. And the Magpie robbed him blind three hours before we arrived."

## IV.

We took Prentice regardless, and the ledger and Corrigan's frightened, careful hand were enough to hang the drowning around his neck where it belonged. But the warehouse's contents were gone, and I braced myself for another mocking card.

There was a card. It was not mocking.

*Return these to the families in the enclosed list,* it read. *I have no use for stolen grief, only for beautiful things fairly stolen. The difference matters to me, Inspector, even if it does not to your courts. C.D.* And folded within it, in that same fine hand, was a list — names, addresses, and beside each the very object taken from it, more complete and more accurate than anything Corrigan's ledger had dared to record.

## V.

We returned every item on the list. Merrow signed the recoveries in her own name and let no report mention where they had truly come from, and I did not argue with her.

"You protected her," I said that night.

"I protected the families," she said. "That her name and their peace happened to lie in the same direction this once is an accident, and I would not count on it twice." She set the card, alone, apart from the others, in the small drawer she kept locked. "She steals what cannot be replaced, Vane, and she is vain, and she is a criminal, and one day I will put her in front of a magistrate and mean it. But she drew a line this week that Prentice never saw and my courts cannot name, and I find I am glad to have read it." She turned down the lamp. "Bright things and patient readers. We are, the two of us, a very small and disreputable library. Good night."
