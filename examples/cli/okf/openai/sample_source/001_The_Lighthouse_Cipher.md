# The Lighthouse Cipher

## I.

To Inspector Ada Merrow she was always the Magpie. I have never heard her name the thief any other way, though the woman's given name — Coral Deveraux — was printed plainly enough in every case file that crossed our desk at Harbour Row. It was not that Merrow admired the crime. It was that she admired the mind behind it, the way a watchmaker admires a rival's movement even while cursing the man who built it. "A dull thief takes what is heavy," she told me once. "The Magpie takes only what is bright, and only what cannot be replaced. That is taste, Vane, and taste is a kind of signature."

I had seen little of Merrow that winter. My own practice had grown, and the fogs off the estuary kept a physician busy. So it was with some surprise that, one raw evening in November, I found her waiting on my step with a telegram folded between two fingers.

"You have your travelling coat," she said, before I could speak. "Good. The Salt Point light has gone dark, the keeper is missing, and the harbour master swears no lamp has failed there in forty years."

## II.

The lighthouse stood at the end of a spit of black rock, and the tide had already begun to close the causeway behind us. Inside, the great lamp was cold. The keeper, a careful old man named Fenwick, had vanished, and yet the door had been bolted from within and the single window was painted shut.

Merrow did not look at the lamp. She looked at the logbook on the table, open to the last entry, and at the neat column of numbers running down its margin — numbers that did not match the dates, the weather, or the hours of the watch.

"He was not writing a log," she said quietly. "He was writing a letter, and he did not trust us to read it plainly." She ran a gloved finger down the column. "Each number is a line of the almanac on that shelf. Fetch it, Vane, and read me the words those lines begin with."

## III.

The message, once we had drawn it out word by word, was short: THE LIGHT HIDES THE SILVER — SHE COMES AT SLACK WATER.

"Fenwick found her cache," Merrow said. "The Magpie has been storing what she steals in the one room in this county no honest soul enters uninvited — a lighthouse lamp room, above the reach of the tide. The keeper discovered it, and rather than fight her he left us a map in a language he trusted only a patient reader to follow."

"Then where is he?"

"Alive, I should think, and hidden by his own good sense until the tide turns." She snapped the almanac shut. "The Magpie will come at slack water to move the silver before the causeway opens. We have perhaps forty minutes. Douse your lantern."

## IV.

She came as the water stilled, a slight figure in oilskins, sure-footed on rock she plainly knew well. She had a lantern hooded to a coin of light and a canvas roll of tools that would have shamed a surgeon. Merrow let her open the lamp room and lift the first bright thing from its hiding place before she spoke.

"It is a very good hiding place," Merrow said from the dark. "I congratulate you on it. I am arresting you all the same."

The Magpie did not startle. She turned, and in the coin of lantern light I saw her smile — the smile, Merrow would say afterward, of a player who has lost one hand and is already counting the cards for the next.

"Inspector Merrow," she said. "You read the old man's letter. I thought you might. That is why I like this county." And then, faster than I can set it down, she pinched out her lantern, and the dark took her.

## V.

We recovered the silver, and the keeper Fenwick walked out of a fisherman's cellar the next morning, sheepish and unharmed. But the Magpie herself was gone, over the rocks and away, and Merrow would not pretend otherwise in her report.

"You are not angry," I observed, on the causeway home.

"I am delighted," she said. "A case that ends in an arrest teaches me what I already knew. A case that ends with the Magpie loose teaches me what I must learn before the next one." She looked back once at the light, burning again now, steady over the black water. "Bright things and patient readers, Vane. Remember that. She will."
